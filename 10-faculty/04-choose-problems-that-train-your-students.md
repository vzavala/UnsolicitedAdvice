# Choose Problems That Train Your Students

> *Unsolicited advice for young Faculty.* Posted on LinkedIn on [July 31, 2024](https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:activity:7224545425532338177/).

In academic research, we have the default mindset that training of PhDs/postdocs provides a mechanism to solve scientific problems. However, I also find it useful to think the reverse way: the solution of scientific problems provides a mechanism to train PhDs/postdocs.

Why does this matter? Because it is important to select scientific problems that will give our trainees adequate training/skills and that will keep them motivated/excited about science during their entire studies and afterwards in their professional careers. 

For instance, if the scientific problem that we select is not ambitious or intellectually-challenging enough, we run the risk that our trainees will get bored and even quit their studies. 

I also think that this has implications in how we select our sources of funding (unfortunately we often do not have control over this). For instance, some funding calls/sources are "mission/application-driven," while some others are "fundamentals-driven"; my concern is that some mission-driven calls lead to "scientific" problems that are too narrow in scope (more development than science) and not particularly intellectually engaging for trainees. Also, the skills that trainees develop when problems are too applied are not necessarily transferable. On the other hand, fundamentals-driven calls can lead to scientific problems that are intellectually more engaging and give transferable skills, but might not have clear societal impact and goals (which can lead to poor sense of direction/purpose of our trainees).

Like anything in life, I think that selecting scientific problems that our trainees will work on requires a careful balance, but we should definitely not forget about how the selection of such problems will impact the training/skills/motivation of our trainees and their future careers (depending on what path they will follow). It has taken me an embarrassing number of years to realize this :)
