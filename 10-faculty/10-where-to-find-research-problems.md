# Where to Find Research Problems

> *Unsolicited advice for young scientists.* Posted on LinkedIn on [January 12, 2024](https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:activity:7151378906061619201/).

One of the most difficult aspects of starting an independent scientific career is identifying suitable problems/areas to work on. 

There are many strategies that you can use to identify such problems (e.g., depending on your research field, your research style, your career stage, your funding situation). Some of the strategies that I have used in the past are the following:

- Listen to senior scientists. Senior scientists have the ability to see how a field is evolving. They have the experience/wisdom that can help you understand the history and context of research areas; they understand what areas might be impactful, which ones are growing, which ones are dying, an so on. For instance, the first problem that I decided to work on in my independent career came as a suggestion from a senior scientist. 

- Listen to your community. Pay attention to some of general areas and questions that your community is looking at. Yes, you can identify your own unique angle to work on such areas, but I think that having some degree of alignment with what the community is paying attention to is important if you want to have impact. 

- Listen to society. Read the news and pay attention to what are the challenges that society is facing (e.g., poverty, addiction, pollution). Try to find how you can contribute to address such problems. For instance, at some point I decided to work on pollution issues of the dairy industry because this is an important issue that Wisconsin is facing. 

- Listen to industry. Industry folks are facing real problems on a daily basis; they can give you clear ideas on what are the problems that need to be solved to make an impact. I have worked on so many problems that have been directly inspired by problems that industry is facing. 

- Listen to the government. Funding opportunities are not random; such opportunities are designed based on consensus of the scientific community, government, and industry. When you see a funding call on a specific subject, such call has been designed by taking feedback of the scientific community into consideration. 

- Listen to your gut. Ultimately, you should trust yourself and follow your instinct. You should work on things that you are passionate about. There is nothing more rewarding than identifying a new and impactful area based entirely on your own instinct. This approach is, of course, more risky (e.g., funding mechanisms might not be well established)... but, in my experience, you can always find ways to establish a funding strategy for new and risky directions. 

- Listen to your collaborators. Be open to work with people from diverse fields; collaborations will help you identify areas that perhaps you have not thought about. Your collaborators might see a unique way in which your skillset can be used. Sometimes you do not have to find problems to work on, the problems will find you :)
