# Mentoring as Feedback Control

> *Unsolicited advice for young Faculty.* Posted on LinkedIn on [November 26, 2023](https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:activity:7134578138507878400/).

Witnessing the growth of your PhD trainees is one of the greatest pleasures of being a Professor. Promoting growth requires of careful planning, monitoring, and feedback. There are many strategies/philosophies for doing this, as mentoring is also one of the most challenging aspects of being a Professor. 

I like to think about mentoring as a feedback control framework, perhaps because this is one of my areas of research :) 

At the beginning I try to give a clear target/set-point of where the trainee needs to be and try to monitor progress frequently to ensure that things are moving in the right direction. In my opinion, the main purpose of the first phase of the PhD is about acquiring skills to solve problems and not solving problems per se (it is about growth). I do not think that it is a good idea to do "risky" exploration at the beginning of a PhD, as the trainee might not have yet adequate tools to diagnose and deal with problems and this can lead to frustration and loss of motivation. 

At some point (typically 2-3 years) you will begin to witness exponential growth in the skills of the trainee. This often comes with an outburst of confidence and productivity. This is where I begin to shift from giving set-points to giving "operating regions" or "flexibility regions"; in other words, I begin to give increasing levels of freedom to do more risky exploration and to apply the skills gained to identify/solve problems. Imposing operating regions is important because, when trainees are growing exponentially fast, it is also easy to get overly confident and "overshoot." For instance, when one is growing fast and getting confident, one can begin to explore many different directions and this can eventually lead to burnout or loss of sense of direction. 

By the time that a trainee reaches the last phase of their PhD, they should be able to be as independent/autonomous as possible. At this point, the trainee should also be capable of continuing their own learning/growth and setting up their own operating regions, as there is no more "upper level" management giving directions. 

Of course, when it comes to mentoring, it is important to recognize that every trainee is different and recipes do not apply, but I think it is important to have a general framework that helps you plan, detect, and address issues.
