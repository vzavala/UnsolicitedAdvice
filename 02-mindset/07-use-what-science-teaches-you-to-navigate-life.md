# Use What Science Teaches You to Navigate Life

> *Unsolicited advice for young scientists.* Posted on LinkedIn on [January 5, 2023](https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:activity:7016774085208657920/).

Use what your research has taught you to navigate your life (professionally and personally). Science gives you a way to think about and navigate the world as well as tools to deal with complexity and to solve problems.

Example: When I was a brand new PhD student, I used to get super anxious with the massive amount of literature that was available; it felt that there was no way for me to read it all and to contribute something meaningful to the field. Didn’t know where to start. I was facing too much uncertainty, and that made me anxious. My response was to read as much as possible; yes, that was a good short-term strategy, but it was not sustainable. I read a lot of things that I did not need to, I wasted too much time and brain power (energy). Over time, I learned to get more advice and guidance from senior scientists, and this helped me narrow down my search. Also, as I became more experienced, it was easier for me to anticipate what was useful or not. 

Now that I know a bit about machine learning, I see this problem as a data collection problem, analogous to Bayesian Optimization (which is a research area that I have been working on). You need to use *prior* knowledge (from senior scientists) that help you narrow down your search; you need to carefully budget your energy/time to *explore* the literature and acquire knowledge and, when you feel ready, *exploit* that knowledge to come up with new ideas/knowledge. As you reinforce your knowledge and can predict potential impact, you will become more strategic in selecting what to read; you repeat this until you converge (e.g., writing your own papers and solve your research problems). This a simplistic view of the problem, but I think it helps you frame how to navigate it.

There are so many examples on how to use science in your daily life; identifying those uses is a good skill to develop, as it will help you better explain your science (in simple terms) and its relevance.
