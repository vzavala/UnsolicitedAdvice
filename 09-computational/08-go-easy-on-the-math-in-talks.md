# Go Easy on the Math in Talks

> *Unsolicited advice for young scientists doing computational research.* Posted on LinkedIn on [August 14, 2024](https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:activity:7229548741672808448/).

Think very carefully/strategically about how much math you want to include in your presentations. It is very easy for an audience to get overwhelmed by math (even for a highly technical audience). 

In my opinion, the purpose of the math discussed in a presentation is to give a quick idea of the nature of the models/analysis that you are conducting; people that want more details can consult your papers or chat with you after the presentation. 

Instead of throwing in all your equations and theorems, you can instead add figures/graphs/diagrams to illustrate the key concepts. 

Also, you do not need to be super precise when giving a presentation; you can add simplified versions of your equations, theorems, proofs that capture the essence of what you are trying to communicate. Again, if the audience wants details, they can consult the papers or ask questions. 

Nowadays I have taken it as a personal challenge to communicate my ideas with the least amount of math possible; I have found that this is indeed very challenging. This is because math is a language, and finding alternative ways to communicate require proper translation. 

Sometimes I also like to joke that I present equations as "eye candy" for the math lovers; what I mean by this is that an equation can be used as a visual mechanism of engage the audience or to quickly send a message.

Of course, the amount of mathematical detail included in a presentation depends on the audience that you are targeting but, overall, I feel that we should be mindful about going too deep into the math details. It is very difficult to keep an audience engaged/stimulated with just equations.
