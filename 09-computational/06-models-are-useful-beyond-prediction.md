# Models Are Useful Beyond Prediction

> *Unsolicited advice for young scientists doing computational modeling.* Posted on LinkedIn on [January 5, 2024](https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:activity:7149030003706331136/).

There is a common perception that a model is only useful when it can predict the behavior of a “real” system or match experimental data. I think this perception is narrow and can ultimately promote a disconnect between experimental and computational research. 

By collaborating with experimental colleagues and modelers from diverse fields, I have learned to appreciate the broad set of benefits that a model provides.

The process of developing a model provides a conceptual framework that helps you think, understand, and rationalize the elements/mechanisms that govern a system. Modeling also helps you understand what aspects of a system are known, unknown, observable, unobservable, and so on. 

Modeling helps you imagine/hypothesize how a system might behave, even if you do not have access to experimental data. For instance, data might not be accesible at certain scales (atomistic or societal level). In some instances, experimental data might be of poor quality due to limits of instrumentation, and the model can help you reconcile or make sense of such data. 

Models help guide data collection; a basic model can help you understand what aspects of a system are most relevant (thus narrowing down the experimental design space) and can give you targets that you should be hitting (or expecting) in your experiments. 

Overall, I feel that the modeling process (and not just the model itself) brings substantial benefits to science. I also find it super rewarding when our models help our experimental colleagues. 

I guess George Box was correct when he said that: “All models are wrong, but some are useful.”
