- **URL:** https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:activity:7510382342465830912/
- **Images/attachments:** None (text only)

> The best courses that I have taken throughout my life (going back even to elementary school) were those in which the instructor forced us to think differently. 
>
> It was not the courses that were full of information/facts, it was the courses that taught me how to analyze information differently... how to ask questions, how to navigate the complexity, how to get to an answer, how to think critically...
>
> When someone forces you to think differently, your brain is being rewired. Some courses make your head hurt, because your brain is literally rewiring. It is like a set of muscles that you do not use often. I still remember taking statistics the first time; it was really hard to think in probabilistic terms, it will give me headaches... but after some time it becomes easier and natural. 
>
> So think about education/learning as a gym for the mind. You want courses that challenge you. Our brains are extremely flexible, but can become rigid if we do not diversify our ways of thinking, if we do not learn new things, and if we do not develop new neural pathways.
>

### 5. 2026-06-01 — The Let-Down Effect: Anxiety After Stress

- **Date:** 2026-06-01 (14:36 UTC)
- **URL:** https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:activity:7467222622519529473/
- **Images/attachments:** None (text only)

> Since early May I have been experiencing some significant anxiety/panic episodes.  I have experienced these every year (over the past 8 years) and they normally appear as the academic year ends and you start relaxing and entering the summer session. Fortunately the episodes are now fading away, but it has taken nearly one month and a lot of hard work to go back to normal. 
>
> The episodes I have experienced this year were strong enough that this prompted me to do a bit more research on their origin. Turns out that this is the result of major swings in cortisol levels when one shifts from a high stress state to a low stress state. This shift is common among faculty and teachers, as our schedules have strong seasonal shifts. But this is also common among professionals that have periods of intense and focused work (e.g., projects). This phenomenon is known as the "Let-Down Effect."
>
> One way that these anxiety episodes manifest is worrying *obsessively* about minor issues. The amygdala (the part of the brain responsible for emotional processing, especially fear/anxiety and responsible for threat detection) becomes hyperactive. When our stress levels decrease, the amygdala will literally imagine/manufacture problems for you to solve. This shifts the energy of the brain away from the prefrontal cortex (the logical part of the brain responsible for assessing if threats are real or not) to the amygdala. The  neocortex acts as the "brakes" on the amygdala, inhibiting impulsive emotional outbursts and allowing for rational decision-making instead of raw emotional reactivity. When the amygdala dominates, minor or imaginary threats feel real and the consequences can feel catastrophic; for instance, in the past I would start worrying for weeks about a minor mistake I made in a paper and I would worry that this would tank my career. 
>
> I decided to write this openly because I know that several people face this issue and I also know that many people do not understand the origin of this. In the past I would face these attacks and try to regulate them using exercise but I did not understand the exact origin. Once you understand the origin, you realize that this is a natural response of our bodies to stress swings. It is not that you are "crazy" or "seeing things"; it is your brain in an imbalanced and transitory state. Some of these episodes feel scary at times, as you feel you have no control of yourself. Speaking openly about these issues is important to normalize and demystify mental health.
>
> #academia

### 13. 2025-12-04 — Complexity vs. Insight in Optimization Models

- **Date:** 2025-12-04 (13:45 UTC)
- **URL:** https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:activity:7402342389698527232/
- **Images/attachments:** None (text only)

> It is always tempting to build a stochastic + multi-objective + mixed-integer +  nonlinear + multi-scale optimization model. 
>
> But, even if we could solve this model, the question is: What insight(s) will you gain from such a model?
>
> I often feel that we push for complexity more than what we push for understanding. I am guilty as charged; earlier in my career I wanted to solve the most complex problems that I could find.  
>
> But over time I have realized that the real value of an optimization model is ultimately the insight. This is not to say that we should not strive to solve more complex models (as those actually appear in real applications), but at some point complexity will start blurring the insight gained and will hinder explainability/interpretability.  What does this optimal solution even mean? How do I explain this to a manager/operator/taxpayer? What is the system actually doing? Then we spend as much time analyzing/explaining the solution as we spend time building the model. 
>
> I still wonder about how to break this vicious cycle of complexity vs. interpretability. Perhaps the answer is to build a hierarchy of problems of different complexity; this can be helpful in identifying what features are truly critical and to identify a minimum working unit. But this still remains an art and requires significant expert/domain knowledge; I hope emerging tools of AI can help facilitate this.
>
> #optimization #systemsengineering

### 14. 2025-11-28 — What Makes the PhD So Challenging

- **Date:** 2025-11-28 (16:38 UTC)
- **URL:** https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:activity:7400211402973696000/
- **Images/attachments:** None (text only)

> I continue to think about what makes the PhD such a unique and challenging experience? 
>
> I often think about this because I want to develop strategies to support grad students. 
>
> As I reflect back on my own PhD journey and that of the grad students that I have interacted with, I have identified some salient features.
>
> - Scientific research naturally pushes the boundary of knowledge and aims to explore the unknown. To discover something new/unknown, you need to truly master what is already known. This requires lots of study, steep learning, and intellectual horsepower. 
>
> - Scientific research is trying to answer deep and difficult questions. Nobody knows the answer to them (otherwise it wouldn't be research). The difficulty and open-ended nature of such questions introduces significant uncertainty; there are many paths you can take and the outcomes are uncertain. It is important to learn to carefully plan and cope with uncertainty/risk. 
>
> - Scientific research requires lots of experimentation and lots of failure. You need to get used to trying and failing constantly. You need to learn to be persistent and to treat failure as learning. 
>
> Dealing with such challenges is not always easy for young people; if I look back at my own journey, I worked *really* hard in my research project. I failed often and went through some "down" periods in which I questioned whether I was capable of contributing new things. 
>
> It is important to have good mentors that help you navigate these challenges; this is why I think that *mentorship* is a cornerstone of scientific research.
>
> #phdresearch

> As a computational researcher, I often do not directly commercialize the models, theory/ideas, and software that we develop. In fact, I am a strong believer in open science, but in some occasions our work has helped support patents and is used by companies.

> Science is a community that generates, exchanges, and refines ideas; we support one another and sometimes we do not expect anything in return... we often just want to help solve problems and ultimately help society.

> Needless to say, there are computational researchers that are incredibly successful in commercializing their work; I admire that ability tremendously!

### 28. 2025-07-01 — Build a Research Program Around a Core

- **Date:** 2025-07-01 (12:17 UTC)
- **URL:** https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:activity:7345787713700143104/
- **Images/attachments:** None (text only)

> Yesterday I was helping one of my trainees frame her future research program. 
>
> One thing that I have learned working at the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) labs is how to build a research program that meets long-term and short-term research goals.

> How can "systems thinking" help mentorship? 
>
> In one of his books, Richard Feynman describes how, during the Manhattan project, computing personnel that he was directing started showing signs of exhaustion and lack of productivity. 
>
> This was largely driven by the fact that the project was, well, top secret and thus personnel started losing sense of purpose. He also described how, by providing basic info on the relevance of the project to the personnel, they started feeling more motivated and their productivity improved substantially. 
>
> This anecdote helped me realize why it is important to use "systems thinking" when mentoring people. Reminding people how their work fits in the larger scope of things helps them feel appreciated and helps them feel included. Reminding people how their work connects to broader initiatives can help them understand the relevance of collaboration, emotional intelligence, and teamwork. Telling people about "systemic" obstacles/constraints that might be affecting their work/wellbeing can help them identify strategies to navigate such hidden obstacles or mitigate the effect of such obstacles. 
>

### 47. 2023-04-30 — Learning to Cope with Criticism

- **Date:** 2023-04-30 (01:16 UTC)
- **URL:** https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:activity:7058247665305731073/
- **Images/attachments:** None (text only)

> One of the most difficult challenges that I have faced in my career is learning how to cope with criticism. 
>
> Being a scientist can be difficult because we face constant scrutiny about our work and ideas (e.g., paper reviews, proposal reviews, lectures, seminars, discussions). Knowing how to take criticism is an essential skill that will help you grow professionally/personally and that will help you develop coping mechanisms to maintain your mental health (e.g., stay motivated, avoid impostor syndrome, or depression). I still struggle with taking criticism and I will perhaps always struggle; however, I have gotten so much better over time and have learned how to make the most out of it. 
>
> Everyone is different and have different coping mechanisms; some things that I try to remind myself of are the following:
>
> - You win nothing by getting upset; no matter how harsh the criticism is, you should try to extract the best out of it and use it for personal gain. Sometimes it is not easy but, by focusing on the message (and not on the way the message is being delivered), you can filter out negativity and extract value. 
>
> - Learn to admit when you are wrong. This is a difficult one because we want to avoid feelings of shame, we do not want to lose our reputation, or we do not want people to "discover" that we are "impostors." Remember that being wrong or failing is part of your growth process. Once you learn to admit that you can be wrong, you will be more receptive of criticism. Everyone makes mistakes.
>
> - Build a network of trusted advisors/mentors. Learn to take criticism from people that you know and trust first, because you know that they have your back and you can be vulnerable with them. This will help you develop mechanisms to cope with criticism. 
>
> - You cannot please everyone; you should try to stay true to your values and to you approach to doing things. It is OK if some people do not agree with your work or approach, it is part of science and it is part of living in a diverse world. 
>
> - Do not take things personal; it is easy to project ourselves in our work (because it is our work!) but remember that criticism is often directed towards the work (and not towards you). 
>
> #phdresearch #science
