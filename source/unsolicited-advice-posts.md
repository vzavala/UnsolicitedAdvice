> Unsolicited advice for young scientists: 
>
> I have said it before, but I will say it again. Your brain is your most important tool and your most powerful weapon. Take care of it by all means possible. This is particularly important in science, because we are constantly pushing our brains to the limit (it is an intellectually draining job). 
>
> - Rest as much as possible. Stop working when feeling overwhelmed and sleep well.
> - Diversify your activities. Try to get outside of your head and promote activities/hobbies that help you stay present and relaxed. 
> - Diversify your skillset. Try to look for new ways of thinking and for new/challenging problems. This helps diversify your neural pathways and helps prevent getting "stuck" in mental loops.
> - Monitor your mental health. Your brain can do weird things when it is tired/overwhelmed. 
>

> Unsolicited advice for young scientists:
>
> A highly underrated ability is that of *self-awareness.* Self-awareness is the ability to monitor our emotions/actions and the impact that they have on other people. 
>
> In my view, self-awareness is at the core of our ability to form, coordinate, and participate in teams of people. Self-awareness helps build empathy, helps understand why we are doing what we are doing , helps understand how our actions can hinder or stimulate other people, helps monitor the well-being of ourselves and of our colleagues, and ultimately helps us understand how to best help and mentor people. 
>
> Self-awareness is also important to help keep our egos in check and helps us monitor and prevent unhealthy feelings and actions (e.g., extreme competition, insecurities, bullying, microagressions). In many cases, unhealthy actions are unintentional, but the fact that there is no intent does not mean that there is no impact on others. This is why it is important to be self-aware and think about how our actions affect others. 
>
> More broadly, I feel that emotional intelligence is a highly underrated skill. Sometimes people are not successful because they lack technical ability, but because they cannot relate and work with others. 
>
>

> Unsolicited advice for young scientists:
>
> Question everything and everyone. 
>
> When I was a student, I used to take things written in books and papers as "sacred truths" that should not be questioned. As you become more experienced and specialized in a subject, you realize that many established ideas, results, methods, and dogmas are either incomplete, no longer relevant, or incorrect. This is not necessarily intentional... in science, everything is in a constant state of evolution; results are continuously being challenged/revisited as more information is collected and as more people are looking into the same problem. 
>
> Some ideas and ways of thinking will persist for many years despite constant scrutiny and some of them will fade away quickly; as such, it is important to scrutinize ideas to test how robust/relevant/applicable they are. 
>
> Challenging ideas also makes them stronger, as it helps people find better arguments and evidence that they are worthwhile exploring. Do not be afraid if someone challenges your ideas; sometimes it feels scary that people question you, but this ultimately helps you develop strategies to communicate your ideas, to defend them, to advocate for them, and to ensure that they will persist.

Sitting down for 1 hr generating and evaluating ideas can do a lot for us; it helps us go deep and cut through the noise to identify meaningful directions. Grabbing a notepad and brainstorming ideas is healthy and helps us foster/reinforce our creative thinking skills. Generating ideas is a skill that you can develop and there is science behind this. > Unsolicited advice for young scientists: 
>
> Working as a Teaching Assistant (TA) is a rather unique and excellent professional development opportunity that can help you acquire diverse skills (even if you are not considering a career in academia): 
>
> - You learn to communicate complex concepts to younger students; this helps you develop communication skills. To effectively communicate concepts to younger students, it is necessary for you to try to think like them and to put yourself in their shoes (this is an important skill to have as a scientist).
>
> - You are serving as a role model and mentor for younger students; students will look up to you as someone that is knowledgeable and that can provide advice about the course and beyond what is being discussed in the course. For example, TAs often advice students on how to navigate their studies and on potential career pathways that they can take (e.g., How did you decide to get a PhD? and What industry experiences did you have as a student?). 
>
> - You learn a lot about how people think and absorb information by grading homework assignments and exams. This gives you a holistic view of what concepts are more challenging/easier to absorb and of how people think and learn differently. Understanding this diversity is useful when communicating concepts (e.g., in seminars). 
>
> - You serve as an interface between the instructor and the students; students might feel more comfortable with you to ask questions. You can also help provide emotional support when they are struggling and this support can be more effective when coming from someone from a similar age (as they still see you as a student). 
>
> - You have the ability to rethink concepts that you learned before in school. As an instructor, when I revisit a concept that I learned in school, I see the concept differently. This helps develop an appreciation for the relevance of the concept and sometimes concepts "click" more easily once you have a more holistic view of the world. 
>
> TAs were not common in my undergrad institution in Mexico. Now that I work as an instructor, I do see the tremendous value that they bring to education. 
>
>

> Unsolicited advice for young scientists:
>
> A key skill to develop as a scientist is that of *reductionism.* 
>
> How do you break apart a complex problem/question into smaller and simpler elements?
>
> Regardless if you are an experimentalist or a computational researcher, reductionism allows you to navigate complexity and allows you to gain fundamental insight. It is super easy to get lost in the jungle of a complex problem and, even if you solve the problem, it will be difficult for your to explain the solution unless you have a systematic way to break down the problem.
>
> Reductionism/simplification also allows you to filter out noise or unimportant aspects of a problem and thus helps you identify the core/fundamental aspects of the problem. By identifying those core aspects, it will be easier for you to teach and communicate your science. 
>

### 16. 2025-09-05 — Unsolicited advice for young scientists doing math/computation researc…

- **Date:** 2025-09-05 (12:10 UTC)

> Unsolicited advice for young scientists doing math/computation research: 
>
> Yesterday I was chatting with one of my students about the importance of having consistent and compact mathematical notation.
>
> Math is a language and different types of characters/letters/expressions have well defined uses. For example, some letters are “reserved” for variables, some for parameters, some for sets, some for vectors, and so on…
>
> When a person reads a paper or attends a seminar, the type of character/letter/expression used already provides an idea of what they are looking at and facilitates their understanding. Having compact notation also facilitates absorption of information; a lot of meaning can be conveyed via context and this helps reduce unnecessary aspects of notation. This is exactly the same as in any other language; you can cut down words and people still get the meaning based on context.
>
> Different communities (optimization, controls, physics, stats…) have different styles of notation, while some aspects are rather universal. It is important to be aware of these similarities and differences; the challenge is that there are no fixed rules and most of these patterns you learn by reading papers/books or via your courses. 
>
> You can also come up with your own creative notation (e.g., the Einstein notation); I have seen people use emojis in talks/code! But it is important that the notation consistent, intuitive, engaging, and that it does not deviate much from the norm to the point that it becomes difficult to follow. This is part an art. 
>
> One of the aspects that I enjoy the most when writing a paper or putting together a presentation is to come up with a consistent and compact notation. I try to push the boundary of how economical/compact I can be without losing meaning, as I want the material to be easy to absorb and follow.
>
>

>

> Unsolicited advice for young scientists:
>
> If you are struggling writing that pesky paper, you can try to break this project into "small wins." 
>
> Allocate chunks of time to complete specific parts of your paper; e.g., Today focus on completing the abstract, the next week on figures, the next 2 days focus on completing the introduction, the next 2 days complete methods,... 
>
> Once you complete a specific part... STOP, rest, and celebrate (do not move to the next part right away). 
>
> I feel this strategy keeps you motivated and reduces the psychological burden of thinking about finishing the paper (which is a big and draining project). Also, try to focus all your attention on just writing during your allocated times; I feel it is quite stressful to be writing while doing other things.
>
> I still use this strategy; sometimes I feel overwhelmed of thinking that I have to write an entire paper, so I focus on completing specific parts and try to isolate myself to complete them (the best I can). 
>
> If you are structured and complete your tasks on time, you will get it done much faster than by allocating random chunks of time here and there in your schedule.

> Unsolicited advice for young scientists:
>
> When I was younger I faced significant depression. This is more common than not in society and tends to magnify in scientific environments (e.g., due to sustained periods of isolation, strong competition, high expectations). 
>
> When I was in my PhD, I was fortunate enough to realize that I was depressed and I searched for help at the right time. What was a shock for me was that, according to the doctor, depression likely originated for me when I was in my early teens (I had been dealing with it for over 10 years without me noticing). > Unsolicited advice for young scientists doing computational research:

> Algorithms are much more than frameworks to solve mathematical problems, they are frameworks/paradigms that help you navigate and solve problems that arise in your daily life. 
>
> I like to joke that I live my life as if it was a multi-scale, multi-objective, stochastic optimization problem :)
>
> For instance, I like to think of writing a paper from the perspective of an algorithmic paradigm called "multi-grid", which is widely used for solving multi-scale problems. The idea is to construct a hierarchy of representations of a system, each capturing features at different scales; in the figure below you see finer at the top and coarser at the bottom, but you can also reverse the order with coarser at the top and fine at the bottom (I prefer this view). 
>
> You can find a solution of the top level that captures the coarse features of the problem (gives you the skeleton, if you wish) and then this solution is passed to a lower level problem that aims to correct details/defects. At the lower level, you can decompose the system into subdomains and progressively refine each subdomain (by iterating over each subdomain, and you can refine it by looking at subdomains in different order). 
>
> Writing a paper feels just like this, you can start writing a skeleton/scaffold of the full thing without capturing finer details. Once you have the skeleton, you jump into into different subdomains/sections and refine; you can iterate over the different sections in different order (e.g., beginning->end, end->beginning). > Unsolicited advice for young scientists doing computational modeling. 
>
> When implementing a highly complex model, it is important to develop a strategy to *progressively* build this up, debug it, and validate it. 
>
> Start simple; implement a minimum model that captures some of the initial features of your problem. Make necessary assumptions (e.g., linear behavior, steady-state, ignore constraints) so that you obtain results that you expect. 
>
> Once your initial model yields expected behavior, start to progressively implement features, and do this in a way that you can isolate effects. Try not to make abrupt jumps in model complexity (take baby steps) and try to validate behavior at every step.
>
> Try to avoid implementing the full complex model first; this is asking for trouble. A lot of things can go (and will go) wrong when implementing and running a model, and it will be difficult to identify problems if you start with the complex model. For instance, you need to isolate issues associated with model features from numerical issues. 
>
> Also, by building your model progressively, you will learn more about system behavior and about aspects that influence such behavior. Moreover, it is much easier to do experiments with simple models than with complex ones. 
>
> Overall, I feel that an essential skill in dealing with complexity is to build and analyze things in a modular manner. To do this you need to identify ways to create “surrogates” of varying levels of complexity. This is a highly useful skill in model development (and science in general). 
>
